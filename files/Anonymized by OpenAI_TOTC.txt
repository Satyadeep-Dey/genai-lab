Title: A Chronicle of Two Cities  
Author: Henry Larkson  

A Chronicle of Two Cities is a historical novel by Henry Larkson, first published in 1859. Set in London and Paris before and during the French Revolution, it’s a sweeping story about resurrection, sacrifice, and the effects of social injustice. Here's a detailed explanation of the story and its key characters:

• OVERVIEW
The novel contrasts the chaos and violence of Revolutionary Paris with the relative calm of London, using personal and political events to explore themes like redemption, loyalty, identity, and class struggle.
The famous opening line sets the tone:
“It was the best of times, it was the worst of times…”

• MAJOR CHARACTERS
1. Philippe Duval  
• A French aristocrat by birth (originally named Philippe Evrard) who rejects his corrupt family's legacy.  
• He moves to England to start a new life and becomes a tutor.  
• Honest, moral, and brave.  
• Marries Elise Manet.  
• His heritage haunts him—he is later arrested in France during the Revolution.  

2. Edward Cartwright  
• An English lawyer who starts as a wasted, alcoholic cynic, but is highly intelligent and deeply emotional.  
• Falls in love with Elise Manet but believes he is unworthy of her.  
• Eventually makes the ultimate sacrifice, switching places with Duval at the guillotine to give Elise and her family a chance at happiness.  
• Famous last words: “It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done…”  

3. Elise Manet  
• The daughter of Dr. Manet.  
• Gentle, compassionate, and almost angelic—she’s the emotional heart of the novel.  
• Her love "resurrects" her father from the trauma of prison and gives Cartwright hope and purpose.  

4. Dr. André Manet  
• Elise’s father, unjustly imprisoned in the Bastille for 18 years.  
• Suffers from mental trauma and periodically relapses into obsessive shoemaking (a coping mechanism from prison).  
• Symbol of suffering and recovery.  
• Deeply loves Elise and becomes a central figure in both London and Paris parts of the story.  

5. Madame Lafleur  
• A ruthless revolutionary in Paris.  
• Constantly knitting the names of enemies into her knitting as a secret death list.  
• Seeks vengeance for her family, who were destroyed by the Evrard aristocrats (Philippe Duval’s family).  
• Represents the cold, mechanical violence of revolution when driven by hate.  

6. Henri Lafleur  
• Madame Lafleur’s husband and former servant of Dr. Manet.  
• More moderate than his wife, but still a committed revolutionary.  
• Runs a wine shop that becomes a meeting place for the revolutionaries.  

7. James Lowry  
• A kind-hearted and respectable banker at Tellson’s Bank.  
• A lifelong bachelor and loyal friend to the Manet family.  
• Provides stability, reason, and action when needed.  

8. Miss Pratt  
• Elise’s devoted and fiercely loyal English servant.  
• Strong and courageous—plays a key role in protecting Elise from Madame Lafleur in the climax.

Here is a chapter-by-chapter explanation of A Chronicle of Two Cities, starting with Book the First: Recalled to Life.

BOOK THE FIRST: Recalled to Life  
Chapter 1: The Period  
• Famous opening sentence contrasts the contradictions of the era ("It was the best of times…").  
• Introduces the political and social conditions in England and France in 1775.  
• Sets a grim, uncertain tone with mentions of violence, injustice, and upheaval.  
• Larkson uses satire to criticize both countries' governments and judicial systems.  

Chapter 2: The Mail  
• A mail coach travels from London to Dover through fog and fear of robbery.  
• Passengers are suspicious of one another—a metaphor for the paranoia of the age.  
• A messenger stops the coach to deliver a message to James Lowry: “Recalled to life.”  
• This cryptic message foreshadows the emotional resurrection of Dr. Manet.  

Chapter 3: The Night Shadows  
• Lowry reflects on the phrase “Recalled to life” in a dreamlike meditation.  
• He imagines speaking with a man who has been buried for 18 years—Dr. Manet.  
• Explores identity and transformation: Can someone return to life after such a long darkness?  

Chapter 4: The Preparation  
• Lowry meets Elise Manet in Dover.  
• He gently reveals that her father, whom she thought was dead, is alive and recently released from the Bastille.  
• Elise faints upon hearing this news, overwhelmed by the revelation.  

Chapter 5: The Wine-Shop  
• Scene shifts to the Saint Antoine district in Paris, a center of poverty and revolutionary energy.  
• A wine cask spills in the street; people desperately drink the wine from the ground. Symbolizes hunger and coming bloodshed (wine = blood).  
• We meet Henri Lafleur and Madame Lafleur, who run a wine shop.  
• Lowry and Elise arrive and are taken to see Dr. Manet, who is being sheltered upstairs.  
• Manet is mentally damaged, obsessively making shoes—a skill he learned in prison.  
• Elise approaches him gently, and something in her voice begins to stir recognition in him.

BOOK THE SECOND: The Golden Thread  
This book covers events in London and Paris, several years after Book One. The "Golden Thread" refers metaphorically to Elise Manet, whose love and compassion bind together many lives.

Chapter 1: Five Years Later  
• Set in 1780, five years after Dr. Manet’s release.  
• We are introduced to Tellson’s Bank in London, where James Lowry works.  
• The bank is proud of its old-fashioned, almost brutal traditions—like sending people to death for minor offenses.  
• Lowry mentors a young clerk named Jerry Baxter, a comically gruff character.  
• The chapter prepares us for Philippe Duval’s trial by establishing the rigid, unforgiving tone of English justice.  

Chapter 2: A Sight  
• In a courtroom at the Old Bailey, Philippe Duval is on trial for treason against England.  
• The spectators are bloodthirsty and treat the trial as entertainment.  
• Duval is accused of passing secrets to the French.  
• Elise Manet and Dr. Manet are present as witnesses, which suggests a prior connection.  

Chapter 3: A Disappointment  
• Edward Cartwright, a drunk but clever lawyer, notices that he and Duval look nearly identical.  
• This resemblance introduces doubt about the identity of the man accused, helping to acquit Duval.  
• Stryver, Cartwright’s arrogant boss, takes credit for the legal strategy.  
• Duval is released, but we begin to understand the contrast between the two doubles: Duval (honorable) vs. Cartwright (wasted potential).  

Chapter 4: Congratulatory  
• After the trial, Cartwright and Duval dine together.  
• Cartwright is clearly bitter and self-loathing, despite his role in Duval’s freedom.  
• He shows early signs of his hidden nobility—he's capable of great insight and sacrifice but is emotionally lost.  

Chapter 5: The Jackal  
• Reveals the true working dynamic between Stryver (the “lion”) and Cartwright (the “jackal”).  
• Cartwright does all the intellectual work while Stryver gets the credit.  
• Cartwright reflects privately on his meaningless life and inner emptiness.  
• Begins to hint at unspoken love for Elise Manet.  

Chapter 6: Hundreds of People  
• At the Manet home in Soho, Elise, Dr. Manet, and Lowry enjoy peaceful domestic life.  
• Miss Pratt, Elise’s protective servant, complains that “hundreds of people” come to visit Elise—an exaggeration, but shows her devotion.  
• A footstep motif begins: Elise imagines hearing footsteps in the distance, foreshadowing the coming revolution.  

Chapter 7: Monseigneur in Town  
• Switch to France: we meet the Monseigneur, a symbol of aristocratic decadence.  
• He relies on four men to feed him hot chocolate, emphasizing his laziness and privilege.  
• The nobles are callous and out of touch.  

Chapter 8: Monseigneur in the Country  
• The Marquis St. Evrard (Philippe Duval’s uncle) runs over and kills a peasant child with his carriage.  
• He tosses a coin to the grieving father—a cruel gesture of dismissal.  
• A man on a rooftop (later revealed to be a revolutionary figure) watches silently.  

Chapter 9: The Gorgon’s Head  
• At his countryside chateau, the Marquis is visited by Philippe Duval, who reveals his identity as Evrard’s nephew.  
• Duval condemns his uncle’s cruelty and renounces the family name and property.  
• That night, the Marquis is murdered in bed—stabbed with a note reading “Drive him fast to his tomb. This, from Jacques.”  
• Marks the beginning of active revolutionary justice.  

Chapter 10: Two Promises  
• Back in London, Duval visits Dr. Manet and asks for permission to marry Elise.  
• He promises to reveal his true name and identity on the wedding day.  
• Dr. Manet, though disturbed, agrees. He later relapses into a silent, shoemaking trance.  

Chapter 11: A Companion Picture  
• Stryver also plans to propose to Elise.  
• He consults Cartwright, who sarcastically advises him to reconsider.  
• Stryver's arrogance contrasts sharply with Duval’s humility.  

Chapter 12: The Fellow of No Delicacy  
• Cartwright visits Elise and confesses his deep love.  
• He says he’s unworthy of her but will do anything for her or those she loves.  
• One of the novel’s most emotional scenes.  
• Ends with the famous vow:  
“For you, and for any dear to you, I would do anything…”  

Chapter 13: The Honest Tradesman  
• We return to Jerry Baxter, who turns out to be a resurrection man—he digs up bodies to sell to medical schools.  
• The theme of resurrection is explored literally and ironically here.  
• Jerry is comic relief but also embodies Larkson’s critique of hypocrisy and exploitation.  

Chapter 14: The Night Shadows  
• More about Jerry’s family: his wife prays, which he calls “flopping” and sees as a threat to his profession.  
• The moral contradictions of everyday people are shown again.  

Chapter 15: Knitting  
• Back in Saint Antoine, Madame Lafleur is knitting names of those marked for death.  
• A man named Gaston (whose child was killed by the Marquis) has been executed for killing him.  
• The cruelty of the aristocracy and the mounting rage of the poor are evident.  

Chapter 16: Still Knitting  
• Madame Lafleur adds Philippe Duval (Evrard) to her death registry.  
• John Barsad, an English spy, visits the wine shop, pretending to gather information.  
• Madame Lafleur sees through him but feigns innocence.  

Chapter 17: One Night  
• On the night before her wedding, Elise speaks to her father.  
• Their loving bond is deeply touching.  
• Elise prays that her love will protect them all.  

Chapter 18: Nine Days  
• After Elise and Duval marry, Dr. Manet relapses and becomes mentally vacant for nine days.  
• He resumes shoemaking as in prison.  
• James Lowry helps restore him again, without Elise knowing.  
• Symbolizes the fragility of recovery from trauma.  

Chapter 19: An Opinion  
• Dr. Manet recovers and agrees to stop shoemaking.  
• Lowry discreetly helps him destroy the shoemaking bench.  
• Symbolically lets go of the past.  

Chapter 20: A Plea  
• Cartwright visits Elise after her marriage and asks her to let him remain part of her life as a friend.  
• She agrees, and he becomes a quiet presence in the household.  

Chapter 21: Echoing Footsteps  
• Elise and Philippe have a daughter and a son (the boy later dies).  
• The French Revolution begins; the Bastille is stormed.  
• Lafleur leads the charge, finding Dr. Manet’s old prison cell.  
• The “echoing footsteps” Elise once imagined now symbolize the real march of revolution.  

Chapter 22: The Sea Still Rises  
• The revolution intensifies.  
• A corrupt aristocrat named Foulon is lynched by the mob.  
• Madame Lafleur leads the violence and shows no mercy.  
• The “sea” symbolizes the mob’s unstoppable force.  

Chapter 23: Fire Rises  
• The Evrard chateau is burned down.  
• The people now take revenge on symbols of nobility.  
• The revolutionaries leave a note: “This from Jacques.”  

Chapter 24: Drawn to the Loadstone Rock  
• In England, Duval receives a letter from a former family servant, Gabelle, who’s imprisoned in France.  
• Despite Elise’s fears, Duval feels obligated to go and help him.  
• He returns to France—under a false name, unaware of the danger awaiting him.  
• The chapter title is metaphorical: France is like a magnet pulling Duval toward doom.

BOOK THE THIRD: The Track of a Storm  
This book takes place mostly in Revolutionary France, focusing on imprisonment, vengeance, and ultimate sacrifice. The "storm" is the Reign of Terror—chaos unleashed by the Revolution.

Chapter 1: In Secret  
• Philippe Duval arrives in Paris and is arrested almost immediately as an emigrant aristocrat, despite having renounced his title.  
• He’s taken to La Force prison and held “in secret”, meaning no contact with others.  
• Duval appeals to Dr. Manet’s name, hoping his father-in-law’s past as a Bastille prisoner might help.  
• Ends with Duval feeling totally powerless and isolated.  

Chapter 2: The Grindstone  
• Elise and Dr. Manet arrive in Paris to help Philippe.  
• They witness revolutionaries sharpening their weapons on a grindstone, covered in blood—symbolizing the brutal executions to come.  
• Manet, revered by the people as a “Bastille hero,” believes he can use his influence to save Philippe.  

Chapter 3: The Shadow  
• Madame Lafleur visits Elise coldly, under the pretense of concern.  
• Elise senses danger—Madame is “the shadow”, symbolizing coming death.  
• Cartwright and James Lowry also arrive in Paris, determined to help.  
• Madame Lafleur's motives are becoming clear: her interest in Elise is not sympathy—it’s surveillance.  

Chapter 4: Calm in the Storm  
• Dr. Manet becomes a prison physician, hoping his status will help Philippe.  
• After 15 months, Duval finally gets a trial date.  
• Manet, ironically, gains more power in prison than he ever had outside.  

Chapter 5: The Wood-Sawyer  
• Elise stands at a spot near the prison daily, hoping Philippe might see her.  
• A creepy wood-sawyer mocks her and pretends to saw off heads—hinting at the guillotine.  
• The chapter heightens Elise’s helplessness and the sinister atmosphere.  
• Cartwright arrives and sees Elise secretly; he’s observing and planning.  

Chapter 6: Triumph  
• Duval is finally tried.  
• Dr. Manet defends him with passion, and the crowd cheers when Duval is acquitted.  
• It’s a short-lived victory—the Lafleurs immediately denounce him again.  

Chapter 7: A Knock at the Door  
• That very night, Duval is rearrested, due to a new denunciation by Madame Lafleur, Henri Lafleur, and… someone unnamed.  
• The shock undoes all of Manet’s efforts and throws Elise into despair.  
• Manet, now desperate, is powerless once more.  

Chapter 8: A Hand at Cards  
• Cartwright meets with Barsad, the spy (real name Solomon Pratt—Miss Pratt’s brother).  
• Cartwright blackmails Barsad into helping him get into the prison.  
• This is the start of Cartwright’s secret plan—the full extent is not revealed yet.  
• He also learns that Dr. Manet wrote a letter during his imprisonment—this will be key.  

Chapter 9: The Game Made  
• Duval’s final trial begins.  
• The letter Dr. Manet wrote in prison is read aloud—it damns Philippe.  
• The letter tells how Manet, as a young doctor, was summoned to treat a peasant woman and her dying brother, both abused by two Evrard brothers—one of whom was Philippe’s father.  
• The trauma of that experience led to Manet’s imprisonment.  
• Though Philippe is not responsible, the revolutionaries sentence him to die for his family’s crimes.  

Chapter 10: The Substance of the Shadow  
• The chapter is entirely Dr. Manet’s letter.  
• Reveals the full story: the Evrard brothers (Philippe’s uncle and father) kidnapped and abused a peasant girl and her brother.  
• Manet tried to report it and was thrown into the Bastille to silence him.  
• He wrote the letter and hid it in the wall of his cell.  
• He ends by cursing the Evrards and all their descendants.  

Chapter 11: Dusk  
• Elise is devastated; Philippe prepares for execution.  
• Cartwright visits him in prison and finalizes his plan.  
• Cartwright has Barsad arrange a meeting with Philippe.  
• He has obtained drugs to render Philippe unconscious and plans to switch places with him.  
• Edward Cartwright, who looks exactly like Duval, is ready to take his place at the guillotine.  

Chapter 12: Darkness  
• Cartwright swaps clothes with the drugged Duval.  
• Barsad helps sneak Duval out of the prison, carried in a coach by James Lowry.  
• Cartwright stays behind and takes Duval’s place.  
• Cartwright’s plan is kept secret from Elise and Dr. Manet.  

Chapter 13: Fifty-Two  
• Duval is one of 52 prisoners to be executed that day.  
• Cartwright, in Duval’s place, meets a young seamstress who suspects the truth.  
• They find comfort in each other’s presence.  
• Cartwright’s inner peace is clear—he feels he has finally given meaning to his life.  

Chapter 14: The Knitting Done  
• Miss Pratt is in Paris with Elise.  
• She confronts Madame Lafleur, who plans to arrest Elise and her child.  
• The women struggle physically; Madame Lafleur dies by her own pistol in the scuffle.  
• Miss Pratt is traumatized and deafened by the noise but successfully protects the family’s escape.  

Chapter 15: The Footsteps Die Out Forever  
• The novel ends with Cartwright’s final thoughts as he approaches the guillotine.  
• He imagines the peaceful, prosperous life Elise and Philippe will lead.  
• He also foresees that one day, their child will bear his name and honor his sacrifice.  
• Final, famous line:  
“It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done…”
